# Legal Assessment: Scraping `alameda.citizenrims.com`

**Date:** 2026-08-15
**Question:** Is it lawful to scrape the crime/incident data published at `https://alameda.citizenrims.com/map` and republish it through our own interface?

**Short answer:** Very likely yes, under conditions — but scraping is the *third-best* way to get this data, and the conditions matter more than the headline. See [`data-acquisition-plan.md`](./data-acquisition-plan.md) for the recommended path.

> Not legal advice. This is an engineering-grade risk assessment assembled from public case law and statutes. Before any commercial use, or if you receive a cease-and-desist, get a California attorney.

---

## 1. What the data is, and why that's favorable

CitizenRIMS is a public-facing portal sold by Sun Ridge Systems that publishes, from an agency's RIMS records system: calls for service, recent incidents, historical incident mapping, arrest/booking logs, stolen vehicles, missing persons, most-wanted, daily media bulletins, and RIPA (Racial and Identity Profiling Act) stop data.

Legally, this matters enormously:

- **The records are California public records**, generated by a public agency in the course of public business, and *already affirmatively published* by that agency. This is not a private company's proprietary dataset.
- **The publisher is a government agency acting through a vendor.** A public agency cannot launder a public record behind a vendor's license terms to defeat public access — that is essentially the holding of *County of Santa Clara v. Superior Court* (2009) 170 Cal.App.4th 1301, where the county was ordered to release its GIS basemap without a restrictive license, the court finding **no statutory basis for copyrighting a public record or conditioning its release on a license agreement**.

The vendor is still a private party with its own server and its own ability to assert contract rights. But the *content* is about as unownable as content gets.

## 2. Claim-by-claim risk

### 2.1 Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (18 U.S.C. § 1030) — **Low risk**

*Van Buren v. United States* (2021) adopted a "gates-up-or-down" test for "without authorization." The Ninth Circuit applied it in *hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn* (9th Cir. 2022): a public website has **"no gates to lift or lower in the first place,"** so accessing publicly available data is not access "without authorization" — *even after* the operator sends a cease-and-desist and IP-blocks you. *Sandvig v. Barr* (D.D.C. 2020) reached the same result for research scraping in violation of ToS.

Alameda is in the **Ninth Circuit**, i.e. the most favorable circuit in the country for this. A fully public crime map with no login is the paradigm case.

**Caveat:** this protection is tied to the site *staying* public and you *staying* out of gated areas. Creating accounts, using someone's credentials, or reaching an admin/staff surface flips the gate down and the analysis with it.

### 2.2 Breach of contract (site terms) — **The primary risk**

This is what actually ended hiQ. hiQ won the CFAA fight and then **lost on contract**: in Nov 2022 the district court held hiQ breached LinkedIn's User Agreement, and the case closed in Dec 2022 with a permanent injunction, a **$500,000 judgment**, and hiQ's admission of liability for trespass to chattels and misappropriation. hiQ no longer exists.

The lesson: *"the CFAA doesn't reach it"* is not *"it's legal."* Contract is the live theory.

Your exposure depends entirely on **V2/V3**:

| Scenario | Enforceability | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Clickwrap splash ("I Agree" required) **with** an anti-automation clause | Strong — courts routinely enforce clickwrap | **High.** Do not scrape; use the CPRA route |
| Clickwrap splash, but the text is only a *data-accuracy disclaimer* (the common CitizenRIMS pattern) with no anti-automation/redistribution clause | Enforceable, but there is nothing to breach | **Low** |
| Browsewrap only (footer "Terms" link, no assent) | Weak — inquiry notice is contested and often fails | **Low–moderate** |
| No terms at all | No contract | **Minimal** |

There is a further wrinkle in your favor: even a valid contract term is on shaky ground if the effect is to restrict re-use of records the agency is obligated to disclose. *Santa Clara* points that direction. But you do not want to be the test case — that's a defense, not a plan.

### 2.3 California Penal Code § 502 (CDAFA) — **Low, but the sleeper**

California's computer-crime statute is a genuine concern because the Ninth Circuit has said it **sweeps more broadly than the CFAA**, it has **no minimum-damages threshold**, and it awards **attorney's fees** — which makes it the plaintiff's-choice claim in California. It turns on accessing a computer "knowingly and without permission."

Post-*hiQ*, scraping an open public page is very unlikely to qualify. What *would* create real exposure — and where *Facebook v. Power Ventures* (9th Cir. 2016) is the cautionary precedent — is **circumventing a block after being told to stop**: IP rotation to evade a ban, CAPTCHA-solving, spoofed sessions, or continuing after a C&D. In *Power Ventures* the C&D plus block was what rescinded authorization.

**Operational rule: the moment a block or a C&D arrives, you stop.** That single discipline keeps CDAFA off the table.

### 2.4 Copyright — **Low**

- **Facts aren't copyrightable.** *Feist* (1991): incident dates, types, locations, and coordinates are uncopyrightable facts. A compilation gets only "thin" protection in its original selection and arrangement.
- **Public records:** under *Santa Clara*, a California agency generally cannot assert copyright in public records absent specific statutory authority.
- **What *is* protectable:** the site's HTML/CSS, page design, logos, map tiles (basemap tiles are usually licensed from Esri/Google/Mapbox and are **not** the city's to give), narrative bulletin prose, and **booking photographs**.

**Rule: extract the facts into your own schema. Copy no markup, no styling, no images, no map tiles, and no verbatim bulletin narrative.** Use your own basemap (OpenStreetMap/Carto/Protomaps) under its own license.

### 2.5 Trespass to chattels — **Low if you're polite**

*Intel v. Hamidi* (Cal. 2003) set the California bar high: electronic contact that neither damages nor impairs the functioning of the system is **not** trespass. *eBay v. Bidder's Edge* (N.D. Cal. 2000) went the other way on a bot that continuously hammered eBay's servers.

The line is **actual impairment**, not requests-per-second in the abstract. Conservative rate limiting and off-peak incremental fetching puts you comfortably on the *Hamidi* side. Note the practical stake: this is a **police department's** infrastructure. Degrading it is not just a tort problem.

### 2.6 Downstream privacy and publication risk — **The most underrated category**

Acquiring the data is the easy part. **Republishing it is where a civic project actually gets into trouble**, because you become a publisher of allegations about identifiable people.

- **Arrests are not convictions.** Publishing a durable, searchable, indexed record of an arrest that was later dismissed, declined, or expunged is the core harm here — and the source portal only retains ~30 days, while your database would be forever. Defamation risk is limited if you report accurately and attribute, but the *reputational* harm is real regardless of liability.
- **Booking photos / mugshots.** Penal Code § 13665 bars *law enforcement* from posting booking photos of nonviolent arrestees on social media, and Civil Code § 1798.91.1 targets the mugshot-removal-fee racket. Neither squarely binds you — but the legislative direction is unmistakable. **Recommendation: do not ingest or display mugshots at all.**
- **California Delete Act (Civ. Code § 1798.99.80 et seq.).** If you ever *sell or license* personal information about California residents that you didn't collect directly from them, you may meet the **data broker** definition and owe CPPA registration ($200/day for late registration) plus DROP deletion-request handling. CPPA's enforcement division is actively reviewing registration compliance. **Keeping the project free and non-commercial keeps you clear of this. Monetizing person-level data does not.**
- **CCPA:** information lawfully made available from government records is generally excluded from "personal information," which helps — but that exclusion is doing less work for you the moment you sell.
- **Sealed / expunged / juvenile records** may appear in a feed and later be sealed. Your copy won't self-heal.

## 3. Bottom line

**Scraping the public map for facts, at a courteous rate, for a free civic interface, in the Ninth Circuit, is very likely lawful** — assuming V1–V3 come back clean (no `Disallow`, no clickwrap anti-automation clause).

**But scraping should not be Plan A.** The data is public record. You are entitled to request it directly, in bulk, in machine-readable form, under the California Public Records Act — which gets you *better* data (full fields, full history, no 30-day window, no HTML parsing, no breakage) with *zero* contract risk and a relationship with APD instead of an adversarial posture. See [`data-acquisition-plan.md`](./data-acquisition-plan.md).

## 4. Operating rules — binding on this project if we scrape

These are not suggestions. They are what converts "probably legal" into "defensible," and they should be enforced in code, not in a wiki.

1. **Verify V1–V4 and commit dated evidence** (screenshots + saved `robots.txt` + saved terms) to `docs/evidence/` before the first request.
2. **Obey `robots.txt`.** If the relevant path is disallowed, stop and go the CPRA route.
3. **Do not accept a clickwrap agreement programmatically.** If entry requires clicking "I Agree," an automated client that clicks it has arguably formed and breached the contract. Escalate to legal review instead.
4. **Identify yourself.** Honest `User-Agent` naming the project with a contact URL and email. No browser impersonation.
5. **Rate limit hard.** ≤ 1 request/second, single connection, off-peak, exponential backoff on any 429/5xx. Full backfill once; incremental daily thereafter.
6. **Never circumvent.** No IP rotation, no proxy pools, no CAPTCHA solving, no session spoofing, no authenticated areas.
7. **Kill switch.** On a block, a 403, or any contact from APD or Sun Ridge Systems: **halt immediately**, preserve what's collected, respond, and do not resume without written clearance. This is the single most important rule on the page.
8. **Facts only.** No markup, styling, images, map tiles, or verbatim narrative. Our own schema, our own basemap.
9. **No mugshots. Ever.**
10. **Person-level restraint.** Default to aggregate and geographic analysis. If individual arrest records are displayed: `noindex` them, exclude victim/reporting-party detail, block-level (not exact-address) geocoding for anything sensitive, and publish a takedown path.
11. **Prominent attribution + disclaimer** — source, retrieval timestamp, "not an official record of the Alameda Police Department," "arrests are allegations, not convictions."
12. **Free and non-commercial** unless and until the Delete Act / data-broker question gets a lawyer's sign-off.
13. **Notify APD proactively.** A short "here's what we're building, here's our contact, tell us if you'd rather give us an export" email costs nothing and reframes you from scraper to constituent. It also creates the record that you acted in good faith.
